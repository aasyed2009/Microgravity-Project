OSD 321 - Finding: Arabidopsis seedlings were grown in 4 groups — normal plants on Earth, normal plants on the ISS, plants missing the UPR gene (a stress-response gene) on Earth, and the same gene-missing plants on the ISS. Going to space changed the activity of hundreds of genes in general, for both plant types. But the more interesting part: on Earth, the gene-missing plants already had somewhat different gene activity than normal plants (makes sense, they're missing a gene). In space, though, that difference between the two types mostly disappeared — they ended up looking more similar to each other than they did on the ground. This suggests some other gene or pathway stepped in to cover for the missing UPR gene specifically under spaceflight stress. Real finding.

OSD-480 - Finding: The Arabidopsis Thalania plant was studied with the same procedure including a control group present on Earth
with the normal and the mutant. In this case however the experiment revolved around a attribute of the plant called the statoliths, 
which measure the direction of gravity and cause plants to grow in a specific way. Using a machine on the ISS to change the evnironment to different gravity leves,
the plant was tested to see how it responded to those different levels with a mutation that got rid of the statolith, in means to understand whetheer there was separate system for sensing gravity besides the statoliths. Result: they found evidence that plants do have two separate gravity-sensing systems, not just one — meaning even without statoliths, some other pathway can still partly tell the plant which way is down.

OSD-678 – Finding: Arabidopsis plants were grown on the ISS and compared to ground controls, but this study tested three plant types at once instead of two — two normal varieties (called Col-0 and Ws) and a mutant called phyD, which is missing a gene that lets the plant sense light. They also grew the plants two ways: in regular light and in complete darkness, and compared gene activity in the leaves versus the root tips. Leaves changed a lot more gene activity in response to spaceflight than roots did. For normal plants, light-grown leaves changed about 10 times more genes than dark-grown leaves — meaning light made a huge difference in how the plant reacted to being in space. The light-sensing mutant (phyD) did not follow that same pattern — in light, it was missing many of the genes that normal plants use to adjust to spaceflight, showing that this light-sensing gene really matters for how well a plant copes with space. There was also a small group of genes that changed in all three plant types no matter the lighting, linked to sensing light, fighting off threats, and handling cell stress — but strangely, these same genes were turned up by light and turned down by darkness, or the other way around, depending on the lighting condition. Real finding: light conditions change not just how much, but which genes a plant uses to cope with spaceflight.


New Articles:

1. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10027818/ -
In this article, the scientists acknowledged the fact that plants in spaceflight are influenced by many other things and its not just the gravity component. For example there are things such as weaker gravity, the hardware used, radiation, and other forms of affecting factors. The question that the scientists set to answer was whether gene changes are really caused by spaceflight or by how the experiment was run. So the scientist decided to take 15 Arabidopsis space flight experiments from NASA data set and re-ran all of the raw data through an identical system, where no external factors were different. They found that how study being run mattered more than space itself, and no single gene was changed in either of the studies, indicating not a clear direction. There are six total gene shared across the three studies, which were HSP101, COR78, PRX39, ABS4, AAP2 and MAGL4 and several of them were linked to stress, heat protection, and the cell wall. Broad themes: stress response, photosynthesis, oxidative stress (damage from reactive molecules), and cell wall changes. Possible Limits to the experiment were that the seedlings were relatively new and it was only the Arabidopsis plant with a few samples per experiment, meaning room for error with the smaller data set. 

2. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/astronomy-and-space-sciences/articles/10.3389/fspas.2022.964657/full - 
This article is more review and the scientists didn't run their own experiments, but they instead gathered a bunch of different studies on how genes change when there is less gravity, and then measured which patterns showed up the most. They were trying to answer whether plants responded in terms of their genes, turning on and off in space and what was missing in the research. They found that the changes in the plants stress responses were the most common factor. Also they found that different varieties of the plant respond differently, for example, their leaves and roots had different gene changes, making it hard to tell whether a changes from micro gravity or from possible equipment, error. Things the scientists said are still unknown or missing: how the response unfolds over time, what happens in plants other than Arabidopsis (only three studies used other plants), and tissue-specific changes, since most studies used whole seedlings.
